Enhanced Games: a spectacle is not a controlled experiment

The corrected report documents performances and public claims from the 2026 event. Its most important scientific lesson is about comparison: event results cannot isolate the effects of performance-enhancing drugs.

Armwrestling LAB EditorialEditorial edition · 2026-09-09

The takeaway

Separate the result achieved, its official recognition, an athlete’s disclosure and the causal effect of a substance. Those are four different questions, and an event leaderboard answers only part of the first.

Describe the result precisely

The supplied corrected report, with sources collected through 30 June 2026, describes a field of 42 athletes and one performance faster than the official world-record reference it used. Kristian Gkolomeev’s 20.81 s over 50 m freestyle was compared with 20.88 s. A faster recorded time does not automatically become a ratified world record: rules, equipment and eligibility also matter. The chart is a historical comparison from that report, not a live record table or a claim of official ratification. [1]

Why the event cannot tell us the size of a drug effect

The athletes were not randomly assigned to otherwise comparable enhanced and non-enhanced groups. Their ages, training histories, event specialisation, preparation and previous bests differed. Public disclosures are also not a complete, independently verified record of each participant’s exposure. A self-described non-enhanced athlete winning does not prove that drugs are ineffective. Equally, a winner surpassing a reference time does not identify how much improvement came from a particular substance rather than training, selection, equipment or other conditions. [1]

Controlled evidence answers a narrower question

Bhasin and colleagues’ randomised study compared testosterone or placebo with and without a standardised training programme. It demonstrated that supraphysiological testosterone could increase fat-free mass, muscle size and strength, especially alongside training. That is stronger causal evidence for the studied intervention than an event leaderboard. It is still not a direct estimate of benefits in elite swimming or armwrestling, and fat-free mass must not be treated as identical to pure muscle. This article deliberately does not provide drug-use protocols. [2]

Safety and suspicion require separate evidence

Medical monitoring may detect some problems; it does not establish that a performance-enhancing regimen has a safe upper limit or no long-term consequences. The report explicitly notes incomplete individual exposure and performance-history data. The video’s broader suggestion that all elite athletes must therefore be using drugs is an opinion, not a conclusion supported by these event results. Criticism is strongest when it stays attached to observable facts and clearly states what the comparison cannot resolve. [1, 2]

What this does not tell us

This article evaluates the supplied historical report, not every later development in the event. It does not verify individual drug histories, endorse enhancement or infer undisclosed drug use by named competitors. Reported performances are not automatically officially recognised records.
